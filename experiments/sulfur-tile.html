THE SULFUR TILE

A recipe, then seven ways to draw the table's third element with no emoji. Each drawing is shown at the three sizes the game renders .s at, then dropped in beside the yellow swatches it has to stay distinct from. Costs are measured builds against a 12832 B baseline — 165 elements, 313 recipes, 480 B of headroom.

1-7 jump to an option  ·  C toggle notes

THE RECIPE

+1 B per alternate route, measured

Volcano + Yellow, with Mountain + Yellow and optionally Volcano + Salt as alternates. Every pair below was checked against the 313 recipes in the table today; all of them are free — except Volcano + Water, which already makes Mountain and is the pair most players will try first.

WHY YELLOW IS THE RIGHT SECOND INGREDIENT. Thirteen recipes already use Yellow as "the yellow version of X" — Gold, Sand, Star, Lightning, Cheese, Bee, Banana, Sunflower. A player who has met any two of those reads Volcano + Yellow without a hint, which is the whole test. The picture is real too: native sulfur is the yellow crust that grows around a volcanic vent, so this is not a colour pun, it is where the mineral comes from.

WHY MOUNTAIN + YELLOW IS WORTH THE SECOND ROUTE. Mountain is one of the table's total leaves: two ways in, zero recipes out. Sulfur is the first thing that could give it a job — the same argument Desert gets in the Oasis experiment and Salt gets in the Rust one. And the route is honest: sulfur is mined out of mountains as well as scraped off vents.

Volcano + Salt is the third candidate and the prettiest by lore: salt and sulfur are two of the alchemists' tria prima, and Salt is still a near-leaf with Rust as its only consumer. It is the weakest to guess, though, which is what keeps it third.

ALTERNATES ARE ALMOST FREE. Measured on identical artwork: one route +47 B, two routes +48 B, three routes +54 B. The second costs a byte and the third six, because every ingredient id is a string roadroller has already seen a few lines earlier — an exact repeat costs almost nothing. So how many ways in Sulfur gets is a design question, not a budget one.

  • Where Sulfur goes next, so it is not one more leaf: Gunpowder (Sulfur + Charcoal, 🧨) is the obvious child, and Acid could take a second route from Sulfur + Water — sulfuric acid — for about a byte. Neither is proposed here.
  • Volcano + Water is taken: it makes Mountain.
  • Fire + Yellow, Stone + Yellow and Lava + Yellow are all free and all rejected. Fire and Stone are the two most overloaded ingredients in the table — thirteen recipes each — and none of the three says sulfur rather than hot yellow thing.
  • Sulfur would be the fourth consumer of Volcano, after Mountain and Diamond. That is a well-connected parent, not an overloaded one.

THE CROWDED YELLOW END

The real constraint, in one row. Yellow and Gold are flat fills; Sand is mottled tan. Ash and Charcoal are the grey-faced pair, in because any sulfur-crust-on-dark-rock swatch has to beat them. Rust is here because option 04 borrows its structure. Lava, Volcano and Mountain close the strip — those are the parents, and looking unrelated to them is a cost, not a saving.

01

THE ALCHEMICAL SIGN

+15 B measured

U+1F70D, the sign alchemy actually used for sulfur, set as e. It is the only option that is literally correct, and the only one whose appearance this page cannot promise you: it is a text-presentation codepoint in the Alchemical Symbols block, so no colour emoji font covers it. Whatever it looks like below is your system font's answer, and a missing glyph is tofu.

  • Historically exact — a triangle over a cross is what the sign has meant since the 15th century.
  • It is not an emoji. Every other e in the table resolves in a colour font on every platform the game targets; this one resolves in the UI font or not at all.
  • No glow — the c/bg branch precedes el.e, so it sits dark between Gold and Lava, which both glow.
  • Monochrome by definition, in a game whose subject is colour.
  • Dearer than a flat colour, the reverse of the Rust result: a lone codepoint from a block the table has never touched is a brand-new string, where #d8d81c at least rhymes with eighty other hexes.
02

THE FLAT FILL

+11 B measured

One hex, c:"#d8d81c", no bg — the Yellow and Gold treatment. The cheapest thing on this page, and, in that same reversal, cheaper than the emoji.

  • Cheapest by 4 B, glows, and is perfectly scale-independent.
  • The greenish hue alone does real work — it is visibly neither Yellow nor Gold.
  • It is a plain yellow square in a table that already has two plain yellow squares and a mottled one. At 32px it is a fourth.
  • Sulfur's whole identity is that it is a crystal. A flat fill throws that away to save 37 B.
03

BRIMSTONE POWDER

+38 B measured

The Sand idiom applied straight: three grain dots over a diagonal two-stop ramp, in sulfur yellow. Structurally it is the table's house style for a loose material — which is its strength and its problem.

  • 10 B cheaper than the facets, and textured rather than flat.
  • Milled sulfur really is a powder, so the picture is not wrong.
  • Being the house style is the trap, exactly as it was for Rust: at 32px this is Sand in a different hue, and Sand is three tiles away.
  • The dots are 3% radii — the first thing to disappear at 32px, which is the size the tile is seen at most.
04

CRUST ON BASALT

+52 B measured

Rust's winning move, reapplied: yellow fumarole crust blooming over dark volcanic rock, with one band of bare basalt surviving. Two materials in one swatch, and it names its parent — Volcano — the way Rust names Metal.

  • Shows where sulfur comes from, not just what colour it is.
  • The dark ground buys more separation from Yellow, Gold and Sand than any hue shift can.
  • It spends Rust's distinctiveness. Rust was chosen because nothing else in the table showed one material eating another. Build this and that sentence stops being true, and two blooms-on-a-dark-ground swatches now have to be told apart by hue alone.
  • 4 B dearer than 05b, for a structure the table already owns.
  • If Rust had not shipped, this would win. It is the strongest picture on the page; it is just the second one.
05

FACETS, THE CHEAP WAY

+41 → +66 B measured

Hard-stop linear gradients stacked as flat planes — a bright plane from the top left, a dark one from the bottom right, one specular seam across both. Cheap, because linear-gradient is the string the table is built out of. Three settings below, and then the reason this loses to option 06.

UNLIKE RUST'S THREE STAGES, THESE DO NOT COST THE SAME: +41 B, +48 B, +66 B. 05a is cheapest because its yellows are near-repeats of hexes already in the table; 05b pays 7 B to move the whole swatch toward green; 05c pays another 18 B for a dark matrix wedge — which is where it starts buying option 04's argument at option 04's price.

05a  LEMON — warm, high-key facets +41 B
05b  BRIMSTONE — the mineral hue, one step toward green +48 B
05c  IN MATRIX — the same facets, bedded in dark rock +66 B
  • The cheapest thing on this page that is neither flat nor a borrowed idiom, and the planes survive to 32px because they are large flat areas, not detail.
  • Says mineral without borrowing Rust's structure — the one thing option 04 cannot do.
  • Glows sulfur yellow, so it reads as its own colour beside Gold rather than as a dimmer Yellow.
  • It reads as a lit slab, not a crystal. Two planes and a bright diagonal seam is the shape Metal and Glass already have; only the hue keeps it out of their family. Option 06 is the fix.
  • ~48 B is 10% of the remaining headroom for one tile.
  • 05a is too close to Yellow. The facets are visible but the hue is not doing its share, so at 32px the tile reads as a shaded Yellow rather than as a different substance.
  • 05c half-repeats option 04, so if the dark ground is what is wanted, buy option 04 and get the whole idea instead of a wedge of it — 05c is 14 B dearer than 04 and says less.
06

THE GEM ROSETTE

+65 B measured

One conic-gradient with six hard stops: wedges that all meet at a single point above centre, which is a cut crystal seen from above. Chosen. No conic-gradient appears anywhere in src/ today — this would be the first, and that novelty is most of what the 17 B over option 05 buys.

06b ADDS A CULET — one small radial-gradient highlight where the wedges converge, for +70 B, 5 B more. It sharpens the hero at 110px and is invisible at 32px, which is the wrong way round for a tile mostly seen small. Shown so the 5 B can be judged rather than assumed.

06b  WITH THE CULET HIGHLIGHT +70 B
  • It is the only swatch in the table whose structure has a centre. Every other one is a ramp, a band or a scatter — directional or random. Wedges converging on a point is a shape nothing else here can accidentally look like.
  • Survives to 32px better than the linear planes do: the wedge boundaries are radial, so shrinking the square shrinks them evenly instead of flattening them into one stripe.
  • Says crystal outright, which is the one word neither Yellow, Gold, Sand nor Rust can claim — and it does it without spending Rust's two-materials idea.
  • +65 B — 13.5% of the remaining headroom for one tile, and 17 B of that is paid purely for a string the packer has never seen. That premium disappears if a second element ever wants a conic, and never otherwise.
  • First conic-gradient in the codebase. Support is universal on anything current (Chrome 69+, Safari 12.1+, Firefox 83+), but it is one more construct the page depends on.
  • If the 17 B is not there, 05b is the fallback that keeps most of the idea — it is the same design, drawn with the tools the table already pays for.
07

THE CRYSTAL CLUSTER

+84 B measured

Inline SVG on the 0 0 32 32 viewBox, the matter and prism route: three drawn crystals leaning against each other. The most literal, and by a distance the most expensive thing on this page.

  • Unmistakable at 110px — a mineral specimen, not a colour.
  • Only option that reads as an object rather than a surface, which suits a thing players pick up and combine.
  • +84 B — 18% of the remaining headroom, and only 19 B more than option 06, which is the closest this page comes to a real argument for it.
  • At 32px the three crystals fuse into one outlined blob and it becomes a worse option 06 at a third again the price. Same verdict the SVG got in both earlier experiments, for the same reason.
  • The cluster does not fill a rounded square, so it looks smaller than every swatch beside it.

WHERE THIS COMES OUT

Recipe: Volcano + Yellow, plus Mountain + Yellow. The first is guessable from thirteen precedents and true to where the mineral forms; the second costs one measured byte and turns a total leaf into a parent. Volcano + Salt is a fine third if the tria prima joke is worth six more.

Artwork: option 06, the gem rosette, at +65 B. The reasoning is the Rust reasoning run one step further. Rust won by being the only swatch where two materials are visible at once — so Sulfur cannot win that way without taking it back, which is precisely what option 04 does, and 04 is the better picture of the two only because Rust already proved the trick works. What is still unclaimed is habit: the table has speckle, streak, band, bloom and crust, all of them directional or random, and no structure with a centre at all. A conic gradient is the cheapest way to draw one, and a cut crystal is exactly what Sulfur is.

The 17 B over option 05 is the honest question. It buys the string conic-gradient, which the packer has never seen, and it is worth it because the linear version does not read as a crystal — two planes and a bright seam is what Metal and Glass look like, and hue is then the only thing keeping Sulfur out of their family. Buy the shape, not just the colour.

Fallbacks, in order. 05b at +48 B is the same design drawn with tools the table already pays for — take it if the 17 B is not there, and take 05b over 05a because that 7 B is the difference between "a shaded Yellow" and "a different substance". Option 03 at +38 B keeps a texture, loses the meaning, and lands next to Sand. Option 02 is honest and forgettable. Option 04 is the strongest picture on the page and still the wrong answer, because it is the second one. Option 07 loses at the size the tile is mostly seen, the same verdict the SVG got in both earlier experiments. Option 01 is the one to resist hardest — it is the historically correct answer, it would ship as tofu on some machines, and it glows on none of them.
